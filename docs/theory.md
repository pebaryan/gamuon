## 1. Clifford Algebra Foundations

### 1.1 Definition and Signature

A **Clifford algebra** $\mathrm{Cl}(p,q)$ is an associative algebra generated by $p+q$
orthogonal basis vectors $\{e_1,\dots,e_p,f_1,\dots,f_q\}$ satisfying the **geometric product**
relations:

$$e_i e_j + e_j e_i = 2\delta_{ij}, \quad f_i f_j + f_j f_i = -2\delta_{ij}, \quad e_i f_j + f_j e_i = 0$$

or equivalently, for any vector $v$:

$$v^2 = \|v\|^2 \quad\text{where the metric has } p \text{ positive and } q \text{ negative eigenvalues.}$$

The signature $(p,q)$ determines the geometry:

| Signature $(p,q)$ | Geometric Interpretation | Example Application |
|---|---|---|
| $(n,0)$ | Euclidean geometry | Dense/MLP weight matrices |
| $(3,1)$ | Minkowski spacetime | Physics (special relativity) |
| $(4,1)$ | Conformal geometry | Normalization layers, CGA |
| $(d,d)$ | Split signature | Attention (query-key duality) |

Gamuon operates primarily in $\mathrm{Cl}(n,0)$ for weight matrices, but the
architecture naturally extends to other signatures.

### 1.2 Multivectors and Grade Structure

The geometric product of $k$ orthogonal vectors produces a **grade-$k$ multivector**
(or $k$-blade). The algebra decomposes as a direct sum of grade subspaces:

$$\mathrm{Cl}(p,q) = \bigoplus_{k=0}^{p+q} \mathrm{Cl}^k(p,q)$$

where $\mathrm{Cl}^k(p,q)$ is the space of grade-$k$ elements. The **grade projection**
operator $\langle\cdot\rangle_k$ extracts the grade-$k$ part.

For a square matrix $M \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$, we identify it with a mixed-grade
multivector in $\mathrm{Cl}(n,0)$. The key insight is that under this identification,
the matrix transpose corresponds to **reversion** (grade involution):

$$M^\mathsf{T} \leftrightarrow \tilde{M} \quad\text{(the reverse)}$$

where the reverse reverses the order of geometric products. This allows us to
distinguish grades by their symmetry under transposition:

| Grade | Symmetry under $\cdot^\mathsf{T}$ | Matrix Representation |
|---|---|---|
| 0 (scalar) | Symmetric, isotropic | $\alpha I$ |
| 1 (vector) | — | (not representable as square matrix) |
| 2 (bivector) | **Antisymmetric** | $B = -B^\mathsf{T}$ |
| 3 (trivector) | — | (not representable as square matrix) |
| $k$ | Alternating | Antisymmetric if $k$ odd, symmetric if $k$ even |

Gamuon focuses on the three grades that embed naturally into square matrices:
scalar (grade-0), bivector (grade-2), and the symmetric traceless part which
we interpret as a "strain" (mixed even grades).

### 1.3 The Clifford Group and Versors

The **Clifford group** $\Gamma(p,q)$ consists of all invertible multivectors
$V$ that satisfy:

$$V^{-1} x V \in \mathbb{R}^{p+q} \quad \forall x \in \mathbb{R}^{p+q}$$

Such elements are called **versors**. Every versor can be written as a geometric
product of vectors:

$$V = v_1 v_2 \cdots v_k, \quad v_i \in \mathbb{R}^{p+q}$$

The action of a versor on any multivector $A$ is by **conjugation**:

$$A \mapsto V A \tilde{V}^{-1}$$

For **rotors** (even-grade versors with $R\tilde{R} = 1$), this becomes the
sandwich product:

$$A \mapsto R A \tilde{R}$$

The map $R \mapsto (A \mapsto R A \tilde{R})$ is a double cover of the special
orthogonal group $\mathrm{SO}(p,q)$ — every rotation corresponds to two rotors
($R$ and $-R$).

### 1.4 Why Cl(n,0) for Neural Networks?

A weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ in a neural network represents a
linear transformation between vector spaces. When $m = n$ (square case, which
Gamuon achieves via padding), this transformation lives naturally in the Clifford
algebra $\mathrm{Cl}(n,0)$:

$$W = \sum_{i,j} w_{ij} e_i e_j$$

where $\{e_i\}$ is an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$. The geometric product
structure of $\mathrm{Cl}(n,0)$ provides:

1. **A natural notion of orthogonality** — the Clifford group $\mathrm{Pin}(n)$
   is the set of all orthogonal transformations.
2. **A graded decomposition** — the scalar, bivector, and strain parts of $W$
   correspond to physically distinct geometric operations.
3. **An exact exponential map** — the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{so}(n)$ is precisely
   the space of bivectors, and $\exp: \mathfrak{so}(n) \to \mathrm{Spin}(n)$
   generates rotors.

## 2. Grade Decomposition of the Gradient

### 2.1 The Decomposition Theorem

**Theorem.** Let $G \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ be any square matrix. Then $G$
decomposes uniquely as:

$$G = \underbrace{\frac{\mathrm{tr}(G)}{n} I}_{\text{scalar } \langle G \rangle_0}
    + \underbrace{\frac{G - G^\mathsf{T}}{2}}_{\text{bivector } \langle G \rangle_2}
    + \underbrace{\frac{G + G^\mathsf{T}}{2} - \frac{\mathrm{tr}(G)}{n} I}_{\text{strain } \langle G \rangle_+}$$

**Proof.** Define the three components as above. Then:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle G \rangle_0 + \langle G \rangle_2 + \langle G \rangle_+
&= \frac{\mathrm{tr}(G)}{n}I + \frac{G - G^\mathsf{T}}{2}
   + \frac{G + G^\mathsf{T}}{2} - \frac{\mathrm{tr}(G)}{n}I \\
&= \frac{G - G^\mathsf{T} + G + G^\mathsf{T}}{2} = G.
\end{aligned}
$$

For uniqueness, note that the three subspaces have trivial intersection: the
only matrix that is simultaneously isotropic ($\alpha I$), antisymmetric
($B = -B^\mathsf{T}$), and symmetric traceless ($S = S^\mathsf{T}, \mathrm{tr}(S)=0$)
is the zero matrix. ∎

### 2.2 Orthogonality of Grades

The three grades are orthogonal under the Frobenius inner product
$\langle A, B \rangle = \mathrm{tr}(A^\mathsf{T} B)$:

**Proof.**

- **Scalar vs bivector:** $\langle \alpha I, B \rangle = \alpha \mathrm{tr}(B) = 0$
  since $\mathrm{tr}(B) = 0$ for any antisymmetric matrix.

- **Scalar vs strain:** $\langle \alpha I, S \rangle = \alpha \mathrm{tr}(S) = 0$
  by construction (strain is traceless).

- **Bivector vs strain:** $\langle B, S \rangle = \mathrm{tr}(B^\mathsf{T} S)
  = \mathrm{tr}(-B S) = -\mathrm{tr}(S B) = -\mathrm{tr}(B S)$.
  But $\mathrm{tr}(B S) = \mathrm{tr}(S B)$ by cyclic property,
  so $\langle B, S \rangle = -\langle B, S \rangle = 0$. ∎

This orthogonality is crucial: it means that the gradient's component in each
grade can be optimized independently without interference — the grades are
geometrically decoupled degrees of freedom.

### 2.3 Geometric Interpretation

Each grade corresponds to a distinct geometric operation on a weight matrix $W$:

$$
\begin{array}{lcl}
\text{Scalar: } & W \mapsto (1 - \eta_0) W & \text{(isotropic dilation)} \\[4pt]
\text{Bivector: } & W \mapsto R W R^\mathsf{T} & \text{(rigid rotation)} \\[4pt]
\text{Strain: } & W \mapsto W - \eta_+ S & \text{(shape deformation)}
\end{array}
$$

where $\eta_0, \eta_+$ are learning rates and $R = \exp(\eta_b B)$ is the
rotor generated by the bivector.

| Grade | Degrees of Freedom | Geodesic Motion |
|---|---|---|
| Scalar | 1 | Scale change along the identity |
| Bivector | $n(n-1)/2$ | Rotation in $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ |
| Strain | $n(n+1)/2 - 1$ | Linear flow in the space of ellipsoids |

The sum matches the total degrees of freedom: $1 + n(n-1)/2 + n(n+1)/2 - 1 = n^2$.

## 3. The Bivector Exponential and Rotor Action

### 3.1 The Lie Algebra so(n)

The space of bivectors $\mathfrak{b}(n) = \{B \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n} : B = -B^\mathsf{T}\}$
is isomorphic to the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{so}(n)$ of the special orthogonal group.
The matrix exponential

$$R = \exp(B) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{B^k}{k!}$$

maps $\mathfrak{so}(n)$ onto $\mathrm{SO}(n)$, the group of rotation matrices.
The resulting $R$ satisfies:

$$R^\mathsf{T} R = I, \quad \det(R) = 1$$

The exponential map is **surjective** (every rotation is reachable) but not
**injective** (multiple bivectors map to the same rotation). The kernel is
periodicity: $\exp(B + 2\pi B/\|B\|) = \exp(B)$.

### 3.2 Closed Forms for SO(2) and SO(3)

**SO(2).** A 2×2 bivector has a single degree of freedom $\theta$:

$$B = \begin{pmatrix}0 & \theta \\ -\theta & 0\end{pmatrix}$$

Since $B^2 = -\theta^2 I$, $B^3 = -\theta^2 B$, $B^4 = \theta^4 I$, etc., the
exponential series separates into even and odd powers:

$$\begin{aligned}
\exp(B) &= \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{B^k}{k!} \\
&= I\left(1 - \frac{\theta^2}{2!} + \frac{\theta^4}{4!} - \cdots\right)
   + \frac{B}{\theta}\left(\theta - \frac{\theta^3}{3!} + \frac{\theta^5}{5!} - \cdots\right) \\
&= \cos\theta\, I + \frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}\, B \\
&= \begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & \sin\theta \\ -\sin\theta & \cos\theta\end{pmatrix}
\end{aligned}$$

This gives the canonical SO(2) rotation matrix.

**SO(3) — Rodrigues Formula.** For a 3×3 bivector, extract the rotation vector:

$$B = \begin{pmatrix}
0 & \omega_z & -\omega_y \\
-\omega_z & 0 & \omega_x \\
\omega_y & -\omega_x & 0
\end{pmatrix}$$

Let $\theta = \|\omega\|$. The key identity for the cross-product matrix $K = B/\theta$ is:

$$K^3 = -K$$

This is a special case of the **Cayley–Hamilton theorem** for $3\times 3$
antisymmetric matrices. The exponential series then yields:

$$\begin{aligned}
\exp(B) &= I + \sin\theta\, K + (1 - \cos\theta)\, K^2 \\
\end{aligned}$$

Proof. Expand the series using $K^3 = -K$, which implies
$K^{2m} = (-1)^{m+1} K^2$ for $m \ge 1$ and $K^{2m+1} = (-1)^m K$:

$$\begin{aligned}
\exp(\theta K) &= I + \theta K + \frac{\theta^2}{2!} K^2 + \frac{\theta^3}{3!} K^3 + \frac{\theta^4}{4!} K^4 + \cdots \\
&= I + \left(\theta - \frac{\theta^3}{3!} + \frac{\theta^5}{5!} - \cdots\right) K
   + \left(\frac{\theta^2}{2!} - \frac{\theta^4}{4!} + \frac{\theta^6}{6!} - \cdots\right) K^2 \\
&= I + \sin\theta\, K + (1 - \cos\theta)\, K^2
\end{aligned}$$

This is the **Rodrigues rotation formula**, which is both exact and computationally
efficient (no series truncation error).

### 3.3 The Versor Sandwich as Group Action

Given a rotor $R \in \mathrm{Spin}(n)$ and a weight matrix $W$, the versor
sandwich product:

$$W' = R W R^\mathsf{T}$$

is the natural action of the Clifford group on multivectors. It satisfies:

1. **Linearity:** $R(\alpha W_1 + \beta W_2)R^\mathsf{T} = \alpha R W_1 R^\mathsf{T} + \beta R W_2 R^\mathsf{T}$

2. **Group homomorphism:** $(R_2 R_1) W (R_2 R_1)^\mathsf{T} = R_2 (R_1 W R_1^\mathsf{T}) R_2^\mathsf{T}$

3. **Grade preservation:** If $W$ has grade $k$, then $R W R^\mathsf{T}$ also has grade $k$.

4. **Norm preservation:** $\|R W R^\mathsf{T}\|_F = \|W\|_F$

**Proof of grade preservation.** In the Clifford algebra, a rotor $R$ is an
even-grade element satisfying $R\tilde{R} = 1$. The map $W \mapsto R W \tilde{R}$
is an **inner automorphism** of the algebra. Since the grade of a multivector
is determined by the number of vector factors in its blade expansion, and
conjugation by an even-grade element preserves the parity of the blade grade,
the grade $k$ of $W$ is preserved. ∎

### 3.4 Spectrum Preservation

A crucial property of the versor sandwich is what it does **not** change.
Applying a rotor $R$ to $W$ via the sandwich product preserves the **singular-value
spectrum** of $W$:

$$\sigma_i(R W R^\mathsf{T}) = \sigma_i(W) \quad \forall i$$

**Proof.** Let $W = U \Sigma V^\mathsf{T}$ be the SVD. Then:

$$R W R^\mathsf{T} = (R U) \Sigma (R V)^\mathsf{T}$$

Since $R$ is orthogonal, $R U$ and $R V$ are also orthogonal matrices. Thus
$R W R^\mathsf{T}$ has the same singular values $\Sigma$. ∎

This means that **the bivector (rotor) part of the Gamuon update only rotates
the weight matrix in the space of matrices — it does not change its scale or
shape.** The scalar and strain components are solely responsible for modifying
the singular-value spectrum. This decoupling is unnatural in standard optimizers,
where all updates are additive and mix these effects.

In contrast, the standard Muon update $W \leftarrow W - \eta \cdot \mathrm{sign}(G)$
is additive and does change the singular values of $W$ through the orthogonal
projection term.

## 4. Newton–Schulz as Renormalization Group Flow

### 4.1 The Iteration and Its Fixed Points

The Newton–Schulz iteration:

$$X_{k+1} = \frac{3 X_k - X_k X_k^\mathsf{T} X_k}{2}$$

is classically understood as Newton's method for solving $X^\mathsf{T} X = I$.
But it admits a deeper interpretation as a **renormalization group flow** on the
singular-value spectrum.

Let $X_0 = U \Sigma V^\mathsf{T}$ be the SVD. Because $X_k X_k^\mathsf{T} X_k
= U \Sigma_k^3 V^\mathsf{T}$, the iteration acts independently on each
singular value:

$$\sigma_{k+1} = f(\sigma_k), \qquad f(\sigma) = \frac{3\sigma - \sigma^3}{2}$$

This is a **one-dimensional dynamical system** on the singular values.

### 4.2 Spectral Flow and Stability

**Fixed points.** Solve $\sigma = f(\sigma)$:

$$\sigma = \frac{3\sigma - \sigma^3}{2} \implies 2\sigma = 3\sigma - \sigma^3
\implies \sigma^3 - \sigma = 0 \implies \sigma(\sigma^2 - 1) = 0$$

Hence fixed points at $\sigma \in \{-1, 0, +1\}$.

**Stability analysis.** The derivative is:

$$f'(\sigma) = \frac{3 - 3\sigma^2}{2} = \frac{3}{2}(1 - \sigma^2)$$

| Fixed Point | $f'(\sigma)$ | Stability | Convergence Rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\sigma = +1$ | $0$ | **Superstable** | Quadratic |
| $\sigma = -1$ | $0$ | **Superstable** | Quadratic |
| $\sigma = 0$ | $3/2 > 1$ | **Unstable** | — |

The superstable fixed points at $\sigma = \pm 1$ give **quadratic convergence**
near the solution: the error squares at each step. This is why Muon typically
needs only 5–10 Newton–Schulz iterations.

**Basin of attraction.** The fixed point at $\sigma = 0$ is repelling. The
boundaries of the basin for $\sigma = 1$ are set by $f(\sigma) = 0$:

$$f(\sigma) = 0 \implies \frac{3\sigma - \sigma^3}{2} = 0 \implies \sigma \in \{0, \pm\sqrt{3}\}$$

Since $f(0) = 0$ and $f(\pm\sqrt{3}) = 0$, the interval $(0, \sqrt{3})$ flows
to $+1$, and $(-\sqrt{3}, 0)$ flows to $-1$. Values with $|\sigma| > \sqrt{3}$
diverge:

```
Flow diagram:
                ← ← ←                          → → →
  ──∞ ────────── -√3 ──────── 0 ──────── +√3 ────────────── +∞
                     → → →        ← ← ←      → → →
                    converge     unstable    diverge
                    to -1        to 0
```

This is the origin of the **spectral normalization** trick: dividing $X_0$ by
its largest singular value ensures all singular values lie in $(0, \sqrt{3})$,
guaranteeing convergence.

**Phase portrait as a function of iteration count:**

```
σ₀ = 0.1 → σ₁ = 0.1495 → σ₂ = 0.223 → σ₃ = 0.330 → σ₄ = 0.480
→ σ₅ = 0.666 → σ₆ = 0.850 → σ₇ = 0.962 → σ₈ = 0.996 → σ₉ = 0.999
```

The characteristic S-curve emerges: slow movement near the unstable fixed point,
rapid acceleration through the linear regime, and asymptotic approach to the
superstable fixed point.

### 4.3 Connection to the Versor Potential

The Newton–Schulz iteration can be derived as **gradient descent** on the
**versor potential**:

$$V[X] = \frac{1}{8}\|X X^\mathsf{T} - I\|_F^2$$

**Proof.** The gradient of $V$ with respect to $X$ is:

$$\nabla_X V = \frac{1}{8} \nabla_X \mathrm{tr}\left((X X^\mathsf{T} - I)^2\right)
= \frac{1}{8} \cdot 4 (X X^\mathsf{T} - I) X = \frac{1}{2}(X X^\mathsf{T} X - X)$$

Gradient descent with unit step size directly recovers the Newton–Schulz iteration:

$$X_{k+1} = X_k - \nabla_X V = X_k - \frac{1}{2}(X_k X_k^\mathsf{T} X_k - X_k)
= \frac{3 X_k - X_k X_k^\mathsf{T} X_k}{2}$$

This reveals the Newton–Schulz iteration as **discrete gradient flow of the
versor potential** — the natural energy function measuring deviation from
the Clifford/Pin/Spin group.

### 4.4 Why the Exponential Map is the Integrated Flow

The continuous-time gradient flow of the versor potential is:

$$\frac{dX}{dt} = -\frac{1}{2} \nabla_X V = \frac{X - X X^\mathsf{T} X}{2}$$

For a single singular value $\sigma$, this becomes:

$$\frac{d\sigma}{dt} = \frac{\sigma - \sigma^3}{2} = \frac{\sigma}{2}(1 - \sigma^2)$$

This ODE is exactly solvable:

$$\frac{d\sigma}{\sigma(1-\sigma^2)} = \frac{dt}{2}$$

Using partial fractions:

$$\frac{1}{\sigma(1-\sigma^2)} = \frac{1}{\sigma} + \frac{1}{2(1-\sigma)} - \frac{1}{2(1+\sigma)}$$

Integration yields:

$$\log\left|\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{1-\sigma^2}}\right| = \frac{t}{2} + C$$

So the continuous flow from initial $\sigma_0$ at $t = 0$ is:

$$\sigma(t) = \frac{\sigma_0 e^{t/2}}{\sqrt{1 + \sigma_0^2 (e^t - 1)}}$$

As $t \to \infty$, $\sigma(t) \to 1$ for any $\sigma_0 \in (0, \sqrt{3})$.

The discrete Newton–Schulz iteration approximates this flow with finite
time steps. The **bivector exponential** $R = \exp(\eta B)$ used by Gamuon
is a fundamentally different operation: rather than flowing the singular-value
spectrum toward $\pm 1$, it directly integrates the **Lie algebra** $\mathfrak{so}(n)$
to the **Lie group** $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$, producing an exact rotor in a single
algebraic operation.

## 5. Multivector Momentum as Geometric EMA

### 5.1 Bias Correction on the Clifford Algebra

Gamuon extends Adam's bias-corrected exponential moving averages to each
geometric grade. For grade $g$ with first moment $m_g$ and second moment $v_g$:

$$\begin{aligned}
m_g^{(t)} &= \beta_1 m_g^{(t-1)} + (1 - \beta_1) g^{(t)} \\
v_g^{(t)} &= \beta_2 v_g^{(t-1)} + (1 - \beta_2) (g^{(t)})^2
\end{aligned}$$

with bias correction:

$$\hat{m}_g^{(t)} = \frac{m_g^{(t)}}{1 - \beta_1^t}, \quad
\hat{v}_g^{(t)} = \frac{v_g^{(t)}}{1 - \beta_2^t}$$

The key conceptual departure from standard Adam is that these moments are
**grade-valued tensors**, not scalars. The first moment $m_s$ for the scalar
grade remains isotropic ($\alpha I$), while $m_b$ for the bivector grade is
antisymmetric, and $m_p$ for the strain grade is symmetric traceless.

### 5.2 Per-Grade Learning Rates

The grade decoupling allows independent learning rates $\eta_s, \eta_b, \eta_p$:

| Grade | Update Rule | Geometric Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Scalar | $\Delta W_s = -\eta_s \cdot \hat{m}_s / (\sqrt{\hat{v}_s} + \epsilon)$ | Isotropic contraction/dilation |
| Bivector | $\Delta W_b = R - I$, where $R = \exp(\eta_b \cdot \hat{m}_b / (\sqrt{\hat{v}_b} + \epsilon))$ | Rigid rotation of $W$ |
| Strain | $\Delta W_p = -\eta_p \cdot \hat{m}_p / (\sqrt{\hat{v}_p} + \epsilon)$ | Shape deformation |

This enables practical tuning strategies:

- **Higher $\eta_b$** — encourages the model to explore rotations in weight space
  (useful during warmup or for highly overparameterized layers).
- **Lower $\eta_s$** — reduces isotropic shrinkage, preventing excessive rank
  reduction (useful for transformers where rank matters for attention heads).
- **Zero $\eta_s$** — turns off scalar updates entirely, making the optimization
  strictly isospectral on the rotor part.

## 6. Conformal GA and Normalization Layers

### 6.1 Conformal Geometric Algebra Cl(4,1)

Conformal Geometric Algebra (CGA) embeds Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^d$ into
a 5D space with signature $(4,1)$, adding two null basis vectors:

$$e_0 = \frac{e_- - e_4}{\sqrt{2}} \quad\text{(origin)},\qquad
e_\infty = \frac{e_- + e_4}{\sqrt{2}} \quad\text{(point at infinity)}$$

satisfying $e_0^2 = e_\infty^2 = 0$ and $e_0 \cdot e_\infty = -1$.

A Euclidean point $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ maps to a **null vector**:

$$X = x + \frac{1}{2} x^2 e_\infty + e_0$$

The inner product $X \cdot Y$ is proportional to the squared Euclidean distance
$\|x - y\|^2$, making CGA a **linearization of Euclidean geometry** — all
conformal transformations become versor actions.

### 6.2 LayerNorm as a Conformal Versor

A LayerNorm operation:

$$\text{LN}(x) = \gamma \odot \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma} + \beta$$

decomposes into two conformal versors:

**Translation by $\beta$:**

$$T_\beta = 1 - \frac{1}{2} \beta e_\infty = \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2} \beta e_\infty\right)$$

Maps $x \mapsto x + \beta$ in the conformal embedding.

**Dilation + translation (standardization):**

The standardization $(x - \mu)/\sigma$ is a composition:

$$S(x) = D_{\log\sigma} \circ T_{-\mu}(x)$$

where $D_{\log\sigma} = \exp\left(-\frac{\log\sigma}{2} e_0 \wedge e_\infty\right)$
is the **dilation versor** and $T_{-\mu}$ is the translation versor.

The total LayerNorm versor is:

$$V_{\text{LN}} = T_\beta \cdot D_{\log\gamma} \cdot T_{-\mu}$$

where the learnable parameters $(\gamma, \beta)$ correspond to:

- $\gamma$ → **dilation factor** (bivector $e_0 \wedge e_\infty$)
- $\beta$ → **translation** (null vector $e_\infty$)

### 6.3 The Conformal Muon Principle

A "Conformal Muon" optimizer for normalization layers would operate on the Lie
algebra $\mathfrak{spin}(4,1)$ rather than treating $\gamma$ and $\beta$ as
independent scalars. The gradient of the loss with respect to the conformal
versor parameters defines a bivector in $\mathrm{Cl}(4,1)$:

$$\Omega = \alpha (e_0 \wedge e_\infty) + \beta e_\infty$$

where $\alpha$ is the dilation component (from $\gamma$) and $\beta$ is the
translation component (from $\beta_{\text{bias}}$). The update:

$$\Omega \leftarrow \Omega - \eta \cdot \text{proj}_{\mathfrak{spin}(4,1)}(\nabla_\Omega \mathcal{L})$$

respects the **semidirect product structure** $\mathbb{R}^d \rtimes \mathbb{R}^+$
of the conformal group — translations and dilations do not commute, and a
geometric optimizer correctly captures their non-Abelian nature.

This is the natural extension of the Gamuon philosophy from the orthogonal group
(weight matrices) to the conformal group (normalization layers).

### 6.4 The Affine Group Aff(1) and Its Exponential Map

While the full conformal group $\mathrm{Spin}(4,1)$ is high-dimensional, a
normalization layer's learnable parameters $(\gamma, \beta)$ live in a
**1-dimensional restriction**: the affine group of the real line.

#### 6.4.1 The Affine Group Aff(1)

**Definition.** The affine group $\mathrm{Aff}(1)$ is the set of invertible
affine transformations of $\mathbb{R}$:

$$x \mapsto \gamma x + \beta, \qquad \gamma \in \mathbb{R}^+, \; \beta \in \mathbb{R}$$

It has a faithful $2 \times 2$ matrix representation:

$$
\begin{pmatrix} \gamma & \beta \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad \gamma > 0
$$

where composition is standard matrix multiplication:

$$
\begin{pmatrix} \gamma_2 & \beta_2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} \gamma_1 & \beta_1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} \gamma_2 \gamma_1 & \gamma_2 \beta_1 + \beta_2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
$$

This reveals the semidirect product structure $\mathbb{R} \rtimes \mathbb{R}^+$:
dilations multiply, translations add, and they do **not** commute — applying
$\gamma$ then $\beta$ differs from $\beta$ then $\gamma$ by the factor $\gamma$
on the translation.

#### 6.4.2 Lie Algebra of Aff(1)

The Lie algebra $\mathfrak{aff}(1)$ is spanned by two generators:

$$G_d = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
\quad\text{(dilation)},\qquad
G_t = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
\quad\text{(translation)}$$

Their commutator captures the non-Abelian nature:

$$[G_d, G_t] = G_d G_t - G_t G_d = G_t$$

This is the Lie bracket of the **ax + b** group — the $G_t$ translation
generator is an eigenvector of $\mathrm{ad}_{G_d}$ with eigenvalue $+1$.

Any element of the Lie algebra is parameterized by a dilation coefficient
$a \in \mathbb{R}$ and a translation coefficient $b \in \mathbb{R}$:

$$\Omega = a G_d + b G_t = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

#### 6.4.3 The Exponential Map exp: aff(1) → Aff(1)

We seek the closed form of:

$$\exp(\Omega) = \exp\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
= \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{1}{k!} \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}^k$$

**Powers of Ω.** Compute the first few powers:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Omega^1 &= \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \\[4pt]
\Omega^2 &= \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}^2
= \begin{pmatrix} a^2 & a b \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \\[4pt]
\Omega^3 &= \begin{pmatrix} a \cdot a^2 & a \cdot a b \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} a^3 & a^2 b \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \\[4pt]
\Omega^k &= \begin{pmatrix} a^k & a^{k-1} b \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

This pattern follows from the nilpotence of $G_t$ ($G_t^2 = 0$) and the
diagonal structure of $G_d$.

**Summing the series.** The upper-left entry is a standard exponential series:

$$\sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{a^k}{k!} = e^a$$

The upper-right entry requires a more careful sum:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{1}{k!} a^{k-1} b
&= \frac{b}{a} \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{a^k}{k!} - \frac{b}{a} \cdot \frac{a^0}{0!} \\
&= \frac{b}{a} (e^a - 1)
\end{aligned}
$$

where the $k=0$ term is handled separately: $\Omega^0 = I$ has upper-right
entry $0$, so the sum correctly starts at $k=1$. For $a = 0$, we use the
limit $\frac{e^a - 1}{a} \to 1$, which gives the pure translation matrix.

**Result.** The exponential map is:

$$
\boxed{\;\exp\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} e^a & b \cdot \frac{e^a - 1}{a} \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\;}
$$

with the understanding that $\frac{e^a - 1}{a} \to 1$ when $a = 0$.

#### 6.4.4 Action on (γ, β) Pairs

The affine group acts on the parameter pair $(\gamma, \beta)$ by left
multiplication of the group matrices:

$$
\begin{pmatrix} \gamma' & \beta' \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
= \exp(\Omega) \cdot \begin{pmatrix} \gamma & \beta \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} e^a \cdot \gamma & e^a \cdot \beta + b \cdot \frac{e^a - 1}{a} \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
$$

Extracting the two components gives the update rule implemented in
:func:`_affine_exp`:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\gamma' &= \gamma \cdot e^a \\[4pt]
\beta' &= e^a \cdot \beta + b \cdot \frac{e^a - 1}{a}
\end{aligned}}
$$

**Key properties:**

1. **Dilation is multiplicative:** $\gamma$ is always positive (a natural
   constraint for normalization scales), and $e^a > 0$ preserves sign.

2. **Translation is additive after dilation:** the $e^a$ factor on $\beta$
   reflects the non-Abelian coupling — a dilation step also scales any
   existing translation.

3. **The semidirect product is respected:** $\gamma$ and $\beta$ are not
   independent degrees of freedom; their geometric coupling is captured
   exactly by the affine exponential.

4. **Small-a limit is numerically stable:** when $a \approx 0$,
   $\frac{e^a - 1}{a} \approx 1 + \frac{a}{2} + O(a^2)$, so the update
   reduces to $\beta' \approx \beta + b$ — a pure translation.

#### 6.4.5 Connection Back to CGA Cl(4,1)

The affine group $\mathrm{Aff}(1)$ is isomorphic to the subgroup of the
conformal group $\mathrm{Spin}(4,1)$ generated by:

- **Dilation bivector:** $e_0 \wedge e_\infty$ (generates $G_d$)
- **Translation null vector:** $e_\infty$ (generates $G_t$)

Under this isomorphism, the matrix representation corresponds to the versor
action on the homogeneous coordinates of the conformal embedding. The
exponential map derived above is precisely the restriction of the
Clifford-Lipschitz exponential $\exp: \mathfrak{spin}(4,1) \to \mathrm{Spin}(4,1)$
to the 2-dimensional subalgebra spanned by $\{e_0 \wedge e_\infty, e_\infty\}$.

### 6.5 The ConformalMuon Optimizer Algorithm

With the affine-group exponential map in hand, we can build a complete
optimizer for normalization layers that respects their geometric structure.
The optimizer operates in three stages: (1) mapping Euclidean gradients to
the Lie algebra, (2) accumulating momentum on the Lie algebra coordinates,
and (3) applying the exponential map to update the parameters.

#### 6.5.1 Mapping Gradients to the Lie Algebra

Given a pair $(\gamma, \beta)$ with Euclidean gradients $g_\gamma, g_\beta$,
we need the Lie algebra components $(g_a, g_b)$ of the gradient with respect
to the affine-group parameterization.

For a parameter $\theta$ with update $\theta' = \theta \cdot e^{\eta g_a}$
(dilation coordinates), the standard chain rule gives:

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial a}
= \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \gamma'} \frac{\partial \gamma'}{\partial a}
= g_\gamma \cdot \frac{\partial (\gamma e^a)}{\partial a}\bigg|_{a=0}
= g_\gamma \cdot \gamma e^a \big|_{a=0}
= \gamma \cdot g_\gamma$$

Thus the Lie algebra dilation gradient is:

$$\boxed{g_a = \gamma \odot g_\gamma}$$

where $\odot$ is elementwise multiplication. The translation gradient is
directly the Euclidean gradient of $\beta$:

$$g_b = g_\beta$$

#### 6.5.2 Momentum on the Lie Algebra

The optimizer maintains Adam-style exponential moving averages on the
Lie algebra coordinates $(a, b)$ rather than on the raw $(\gamma, \beta)$
parameters:

$$
\begin{aligned}
m_a^{(t)} &= \beta_1 m_a^{(t-1)} + (1 - \beta_1) g_a^{(t)} \\
v_a^{(t)} &= \beta_2 v_a^{(t-1)} + (1 - \beta_2) (g_a^{(t)})^2 \\[4pt]
m_b^{(t)} &= \beta_1 m_b^{(t-1)} + (1 - \beta_1) g_b^{(t)} \\
v_b^{(t)} &= \beta_2 v_b^{(t-1)} + (1 - \beta_2) (g_b^{(t)})^2
\end{aligned}
$$

With bias correction:

$$
\hat{m}_a = \frac{m_a}{1 - \beta_1^t}, \quad
\hat{v}_a = \frac{v_a}{1 - \beta_2^t}, \quad
\hat{m}_b = \frac{m_b}{1 - \beta_1^t}, \quad
\hat{v}_b = \frac{v_b}{1 - \beta_2^t}
$$

#### 6.5.3 Lie Algebra Step

The Lie algebra step is the standard Adam normalisation:

$$
a = -\eta \cdot \frac{\hat{m}_a}{\sqrt{\hat{v}_a} + \epsilon}, \qquad
b = -\eta \cdot \frac{\hat{m}_b}{\sqrt{\hat{v}_b} + \epsilon}
$$

The negative sign ensures gradient descent (rather than ascent).

#### 6.5.4 Affine Exponential Update

The step $(a, b)$ is integrated into the parameter pair via the affine
group exponential:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\delta_\gamma &= e^a \\
\delta_\beta &= b \cdot \frac{e^a - 1}{a} \quad\text{(with small-a limit)}\\[8pt]
\gamma' &= \gamma \cdot \delta_\gamma \\
\beta' &= \delta_\gamma \cdot \beta + \delta_\beta
\end{aligned}
$$

#### 6.5.5 Complete Algorithm

```
Algorithm: ConformalMuon step for (γ, β) pair
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Require: Learning rate η, betas (β₁, β₂), eps ε
Require: Current parameters (γ, β), Euclidean gradients (g_γ, g_β)
Require: State (step t, momentum buffers)

 1:  g_γ ← g_γ + λ·γ                         ▷ weight decay (if λ > 0)
 2:  g_β ← g_β + λ·β                         ▷ weight decay
 3:  g_a ← γ ⊙ g_γ                            ▷ Lie algebra dilation gradient
 4:  g_b ← g_β                                ▷ Lie algebra translation gradient
 5:  m_a ← β₁·m_a + (1-β₁)·g_a               ▷ first moment (dilation)
 6:  v_a ← β₂·v_a + (1-β₂)·g_a²             ▷ second moment (dilation)
 7:  m_b ← β₁·m_b + (1-β₁)·g_b               ▷ first moment (translation)
 8:  v_b ← β₂·v_b + (1-β₂)·g_b²             ▷ second moment (translation)
 9:  t ← t + 1
10:  a ← -η · m_a/(1-β₁ᵗ) / (√(v_a/(1-β₂ᵗ)) + ε)   ▷ bias-corrected step
11:  b ← -η · m_b/(1-β₁ᵗ) / (√(v_b/(1-β₂ᵗ)) + ε)
12:  δ_γ ← exp(a)                             ▷ affine exponential
13:  trans ← b · (exp(a)-1)/a                 ▷ (limit 1 when a=0)
14:  γ ← γ · δ_γ                              ▷ multiplicative dilation
15:  β ← δ_γ · β + trans                      ▷ coupled translation
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
```

#### 6.5.6 Auto-Detection of Conformal Pairs

In practice, the optimizer needs to discover which parameters form conformal
$(\gamma, \beta)$ pairs. The :func:`find_conformal_pairs` utility scans all
submodules of a model and returns pairs for every normalization layer,
including parameter-free norm types such as RMSNorm and weight-normalized
modules:

```python
def find_conformal_pairs(
    model,
    types=None,
    detect_weightnorm=True,
):
    """Returns list of (weight, bias) tuples for norm layers.

    Also detects weight-normalized modules (weight_g parameters)
    created by torch.nn.utils.weight_norm.
    """
    if types is None:
        types = (
            nn.LayerNorm,
            nn.BatchNorm1d, nn.BatchNorm2d, nn.BatchNorm3d,
            nn.RMSNorm,
        )
    pairs = []
    for module in model.modules():
        if isinstance(module, types):
            w = getattr(module, "weight", None)
            b = getattr(module, "bias", None)
            if w is not None:
                pairs.append((w, b) if b is not None else (w, None))
        # Detect weight_norm parametrizations
        if detect_weightnorm:
            weight_g = getattr(module, "weight_g", None)
            if weight_g is not None and isinstance(weight_g, nn.Parameter):
                is_1d_scale = sum(1 for s in weight_g.shape if s > 1) == 1
                if is_1d_scale:
                    if not isinstance(module, types if types is not None else ()):
                        pairs.append((weight_g, None))
    return pairs
```

**Detection scope.** The function now supports three families of normalization
parameters:

| Family | Modules detected | Bias | Shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Standard norm layers** | ``LayerNorm``, ``BatchNorm1d/2d/3d`` | Optional (`nn.Parameter` or ``None``) | 1‑D |
| **RMS-only norms** | ``RMSNorm`` | Always ``None`` (no bias) | 1‑D |
| **Weight-normalized** | Any module with ``torch.nn.utils.weight_norm`` applied | Always ``None`` (no bias) | Effectively 1‑D (any dimensionality with one non‑singleton axis) |

Detection cascades through all submodules and works for any layer with
$\gamma$ (weight) and optional $\beta$ (bias) 1-D parameters.

**RMSNorm.** Added in PyTorch 2.11, RMSNorm has a learnable ``weight``
parameter (the $\gamma$ scale) but **no bias**. It is paired as
``(weight, None)`` — the optimizer applies the multiplicative dilation update
without an affine translation component.

**Weight normalization.** Modules wrapped with
:func:`torch.nn.utils.weight_norm` acquire a 1‑D ``weight_g`` parameter
representing the per-output scale. This parameter is geometrically identical
to a normalization $\gamma$ and is paired as ``(weight_g, None)``. The
function accepts a ``detect_weightnorm=False`` flag to disable this scan when
only standard norm layers are desired.

The shape check ``sum(1 for s in weight_g.shape if s > 1) == 1`` accepts any
"effectively 1‑D" parameter regardless of dimensionality — Linear layers produce
``(out_features, 1)`` column vectors, while Conv2d produces
``(out_channels, 1, 1, 1)`` 4‑D tensors — both are correctly identified as
conformal scale parameters.

#### 6.5.7 Parameter Group Configuration

The :class:`ConformalMuon` optimizer accepts three modes of parameter
specification:

1. **Module auto-detection** — pass an `nn.Module` directly; conformal pairs
   are detected automatically, and remaining parameters receive SGD fallback.

2. **Tuple list** — pass a list of `(weight, bias)` tuples from
   `find_conformal_pairs`, giving explicit control over which pairs are
   treated conformally.

3. **Standard param groups** — pass standard PyTorch param groups with an
   optional `"is_conformal": True` flag; groups without this flag receive
   SGD fallback.

#### 6.5.8 Comparison: ConformalMuon vs Adam for Norm Layers

| Aspect | Adam | ConformalMuon |
|---|---|---|
| Geometry | Euclidean (flat) | Affine group Aff(1) (curved) |
| Dilation update | Additive: $\gamma \leftarrow \gamma - \eta \cdot \hat{m}_\gamma$ | Multiplicative: $\gamma \leftarrow \gamma \cdot e^{-\eta \cdot \hat{m}_a}$ |
| Translation update | $\beta \leftarrow \beta - \eta \cdot \hat{m}_\beta$ | Coupled: $\beta \leftarrow e^a \cdot \beta + b \cdot (e^a-1)/a$ |
| Parameter coupling | None (independent) | Full (semidirect product) |
| $\gamma$ sign guaranteed | ❌ (can become negative) | ✅ ($e^a > 0$) |
| Momentum | Parameter-space EMA | Lie-algebra EMA |

## 6.6 GamuonAuto: A Unified Meta-Optimizer

### 6.6.1 Motivation

Sections 1–5 developed the geometric update for square weight matrices(Gamuon: $W \mapsto R W R^\mathsf{T} - \eta \cdot \text{strain} - \eta \cdot \text{scalar}$),
while Section 6 developed the conformal-group update for normalization
parameters (ConformalMuon : $(\gamma, \beta) \mapsto (\gamma e^a,\, e^a \beta + b \cdot (e^a-1)/a)$).

In practice, a single model contains *both* types of parameters — and often
a third category (1‑D biases, embeddings, etc.) that don't benefit from
either geometric treatment. `GamuonAuto` is a **meta-optimizer** that
automatically classifies every parameter in an `nn.Module` and dispatches
it to the appropriate geometric or standard optimizer.

### 6.6.2 The Dispatch Rule

Given an `nn.Module`, `GamuonAuto` partitions its parameters into three
groups:

| Category | Detected by | Optimizer | Update Form | Theoretical Basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Norm-layer $(\gamma, \beta)$ pairs** | `find_conformal_pairs(model)` — scans submodules for `nn.LayerNorm`, `nn.BatchNorm*`, `nn.RMSNorm`, `nn.GroupNorm`, and weight-normalized `weight_g` parameters | `ConformalMuon` | $(\gamma, \beta) \mapsto (\gamma e^a,\, e^a \beta + b \cdot \frac{e^a-1}{a})$ | §6.4 — Affine group $\mathrm{Aff}(1)$ exponential |
| **2‑D weight matrices** | Dimensionality check: `p.ndim == 2` and not already assigned to ConformalMuon | `Gamuon` | $W \mapsto R W R^\mathsf{T} - \eta \cdot \text{strain} - \eta \cdot \text{scalar}$ | §2–3 — Grade decomposition in $\mathrm{Cl}(n,0)$ |
| **1‑D / other parameters** | Everything not caught above (biases, embeddings, conv kernels, etc.) | Plain SGD | $\theta \mapsto \theta - \eta \cdot g_\theta$ | Euclidean gradient descent |

The three sub-optimizers are composed via sequential application in each
training step:

```
Algorithm: GamuonAuto.step(closure=None)
──────────────────────────────────────────
 1:  for opt in [ConformalMuon, Gamuon, SGD]:
 2:      if opt is not None:
 3:          opt.step(closure)
 4:          closure = None                        ▷ only first call gets closure
──────────────────────────────────────────
```

This design ensures that **each parameter is updated by exactly one sub-optimizer**
(since the partition is a disjoint cover of the parameter set), and the
API remains a drop-in replacement for `torch.optim.Optimizer`.

### 6.6.3 Why This Partition Is Theoretically Sound

The dispatch rule is not arbitrary — each decision follows from the
algebraic structure of the parameter:

1. **Norm-layer $(\gamma, \beta)$ → ConformalMuon.**
   These parameters form an *affine group* $\mathrm{Aff}(1)$ (see §6.4).
   Applying a Euclidean optimizer treats them as independent scalars,
   ignoring the semidirect-product coupling $[G_d, G_t] = G_t$. The
   conformal exponential correctly handles this coupling and guarantees
   $\gamma > 0$ by construction.

2. **2‑D weight matrices → Gamuon.**
   A matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ is a grade-1 element (linear
   transformation) in the Clifford algebra. When padded to square (or
   natively square), it admits the grade decomposition into scalar +
   bivector + strain (§2). The bivector component generates a rotor
   $R = \exp(\eta B) \in \mathrm{Spin}(n)$, which acts by versor sandwich
   — preserving the singular-value spectrum (§3.4).

3. **1‑D / other → SGD.**
   Scalars, vectors, and higher-order tensors do not embed naturally into
   the grade structure of $\mathrm{Cl}(n,0)$ for square matrices. For
   these, Euclidean gradient descent is the correct null hypothesis.

### 6.6.4 Relation to the Theory of Combined Optimizers

`GamuonAuto` is a concrete instance of a more general principle: **each
parameter's optimizer should be chosen to match its algebraic type.**

| Parameter type | Algebraic structure | Natural optimizer |
|---|---|---|
| Norm-layer $(\gamma, \beta)$ | Affine group $\mathrm{Aff}(1) \cong \mathbb{R} \rtimes \mathbb{R}^+$ | `ConformalMuon` |
| Square weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ | Clifford algebra $\mathrm{Cl}(n,0)$, grade decomposition | `Gamuon` |
| General $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^d$ | Euclidean vector space $\mathbb{R}^d$ | SGD / Adam |

This type-driven dispatch is analogous to how a well-designed deep learning
framework uses different initializations for different layer types: it's
not about finding a single "best" optimizer, but about matching the
optimizer's inductive bias to the parameter's geometry.

### 6.6.5 Practical Considerations

**Orthogonality of parameter groups.** Because each parameter belongs to
exactly one partition, the three sub-optimizers' updates sum without
interference. This is a form of **geometric orthogonality** — not in the
inner-product sense, but in the sense that no parameter receives a compound
update from multiple optimizers.

**Weight decay.** When `weight_decay > 0`, `GamuonAuto` passes it to all
three sub-optimizers. For `ConformalMuon`, weight decay is applied to the
Lie-algebra coordinates; for `Gamuon`, it is absorbed into the scalar grade
(isotropic contraction); for SGD, it is standard L2 regularization. The
theoretical justification for treating weight decay at the parameter level
(rather than inside the exponential map) follows from the decoupling of
grades — weight decay is a scalar operation and does not affect the rotor
or strain updates.

**Bias correction.** Both `Gamuon` and `ConformalMuon` maintain
Adam-style bias-corrected exponential moving averages on their respective
state spaces (grade-valued moments for Gamuon, Lie-algebra moments for
ConformalMuon). `GamuonAuto` inherits the standard `betas` and `eps`
hyperparameters from these sub-optimizers.

### 6.6.6 Proof of Correctness: Equivalence with Manual Composition

For a given model, let $\mathcal{P} = \mathcal{P}_c \sqcup \mathcal{P}_g \sqcup \mathcal{P}_s$
be the disjoint partition induced by `GamuonAuto`, where:
- $\mathcal{P}_c$ = conformal pairs (norm layers)
- $\mathcal{P}_g$ = 2‑D weight matrices
- $\mathcal{P}_s$ = remaining (scalar) parameters

Define the manual combined optimizer $\mathcal{O}_{\text{manual}}$ as:

$$\mathcal{O}_{\text{manual}} = \text{ConformalMuon}(\mathcal{P}_c) \;\circ\; \text{Gamuon}(\mathcal{P}_g) \;\circ\; \text{SGD}(\mathcal{P}_s)$$

Then `GamuonAuto` produces $\mathcal{O}_{\text{auto}}$ such that for any
gradient computation:

$$\mathcal{O}_{\text{auto}}.\text{step}() \equiv \mathcal{O}_{\text{manual}}.\text{step}()$$

**Proof sketch.** Both optimizers iterate over the same three sub-optimizers
in the same order, each operating on the same disjoint parameter sets. Since
sub-optimizer state depends only on their assigned parameters and step count,
the updates are identical by induction on step number. The equivalence is
verified empirically by the benchmark suite (`benchmarks/benchmark_transformer.py`). ∎

## 7. Comparison with Related Optimizers

### 7.1 Muon

The standard [Muon optimizer](https://kellerjordan.github.io/posts/muon/) (Jordan et al., 2024)
projects the gradient $G$ onto the orthogonal group via Newton–Schulz iterations
and applies an additive update:

$$W \leftarrow W - \eta \cdot \mathrm{sign}(G)$$

where $\mathrm{sign}(G) \approx UV^\mathsf{T}$ from the SVD $G = U\Sigma V^\mathsf{T}$.

| Aspect | Muon | Gamuon |
|---|---|---|
| Orthogonal projection | Newton–Schulz (iterative) | $\exp(B)$ (exact) |
| Grade handling | Implicit (all-in-one projection) | Explicit (scalar + bivector + strain) |
| Update form | Additive: $W \leftarrow W - \eta \cdot \text{sign}(G)$ | Sandwich: $W \leftarrow RWR^\mathsf{T} - \text{strain} - \text{scalar}$ |
| Momentum | Standard (scalar per-element) | Grade-aware (6 buffers per param) |
| Computation | $O(n^3)$ for NS iterations | $O(n^3)$ for $\exp(B)$ (same scaling) |
| Non-square | Not natively supported | Automatic padding/unpadding |

### 7.2 Adam

Adam uses per-element adaptive learning rates with first and second moments:

$$W \leftarrow W - \eta \cdot \frac{m}{\sqrt{v} + \epsilon}$$

| Aspect | Adam | Gamuon |
|---|---|---|
| Geometry | Euclidean (flat) | Clifford-algebraic (curved) |
| Update structure | Elementwise independent | Grade-structured (rotation + scale + shape) |
| Momentum | Single EMA | 6 EMAs (3 moments × 3 grades) |
| Invariance | None | Rotor part preserves spectrum |

### 7.3 Shampoo

[Shampoo](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.09568) (Gupta et al., 2018) uses
Kronecker-factored preconditioners:

$$W \leftarrow W - \eta \cdot L^{-1/4} G R^{-1/4}$$

where $L = \sum G G^\mathsf{T}$ and $R = \sum G^\mathsf{T} G$ are the
left and right preconditioners.

| Aspect | Shampoo | Gamuon |
|---|---|---|
| Preconditioning | Kronecker factors $L, R$ | None (uses geometric structure directly) |
| Rationale | Approximate Fisher information | Geometric grade decomposition |
| Computation | $O(n^3)$ for matrix roots | $O(n^3)$ for matrix exponential |
| Grade structure | Implicit | Explicit |

### 7.4 SOAP

[SOAP](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.11321) (Vyas et al., 2024) combines shampoo-style
preconditioning with Adam:

| Aspect | SOAP | Gamuon |
|---|---|---|
| Core operation | Eigen-decomposition of $L, R$ | Matrix exponential of bivector |
| Update | $W \leftarrow W - \eta \cdot U g(V^\mathsf{T} g V) U^\mathsf{T}$ | $W \leftarrow RWR^\mathsf{T} - \text{strain} - \text{scalar}$ |
| Geometric meaning | Whitens in spectral basis of gradients | Rotates in the direction of the bivector gradient |

### 7.5 Riemannian SGD on the Stiefel Manifold

When optimizing on the Stiefel manifold $\mathrm{St}(n, k) = \{W \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k} : W^\mathsf{T} W = I\}$,
Riemannian SGD uses:

$$W \leftarrow \text{retr}_W(-\eta \cdot \text{grad}_W \mathcal{L})$$

where $\text{retr}$ is a retraction (usually QR or Cayley transform).

| Aspect | Stiefel SGD | Gamuon |
|---|---|---|
| Constraint | $W^\mathsf{T} W = I$ | None (unconstrained optimization) |
| Update | Retraction + projection | Sandwich + strain + scalar |
| Geometric structure | Riemannian manifold | Clifford algebra |

## 8. Open Questions and Future Directions

### 8.1 Multi-Grade Coupling

The current Gamuon treats each grade independently. But in the full Clifford
algebra, grades can **interact** through the geometric product. For example,
the commutator of a bivector and a strain produces another bivector:

$$[B, S] = BS - SB \in \mathfrak{b}(n)$$

This suggests that strain updates should feed back into the bivector component,
creating a **coupled grade flow**. Whether this improves optimization is an
open question.

### 8.2 Non-Square Matrices Without Padding

Current Gamuon pads non-square matrices to square, processes, then slices back.
A more principled approach would use the **full Clifford algebra Cl(m, n)** with
mixed signature, where $W \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ is a bivector connecting
two spaces of different dimensions. The exponential map in this setting requires
the **Clifford-Lipschitz group** rather than $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$.

### 8.3 Lorentz Signatures for Attention

Transformer attention matrices $QK^\mathsf{T}$ naturally inhabit the split-signature
algebra $\mathrm{Cl}(d, d)$, where the query and key spaces are dual. Optimization
in this algebra would respect the **light-cone structure** of the attention
mechanism — a potentially significant improvement over treating $Q$ and $K$
as independent matrices.

### 8.4 Stochastic Bivector Exponentials

For very large $n$ (e.g., $n > 2048$), computing $\exp(B)$ via `torch.matrix_exp`
becomes expensive ($O(n^3)$ with large constants). Stochastic approximations:

- **Taylor truncation:** $\exp(B) \approx I + B + B^2/2$ (valid for small $\|B\|$)
- **Cayley transform:** $R = (I + B/2)(I - B/2)^{-1}$ (always orthogonal, $O(n^3)$)
- **Lanczos method:** Krylov subspace approximation to $\exp(B)v$

These could trade accuracy for speed in the large-parameter regime.

### 8.5 Higher-Grade Optimizers

Beyond matrices, higher-order tensors (e.g., convolutional kernels of shape
$c_{\text{out}} \times c_{\text{in}} \times k \times k$) could be optimized
using higher-grade blades in the appropriate Clifford algebra. The grade
decomposition generalizes to tensors of any order, though the exponential map
becomes more complex.
